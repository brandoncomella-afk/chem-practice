Types of Bonds

Question 1Beginner

In a covalent bond, two atoms share electrons. A covalent bond generally occurs between:

  1. A. two metals, such as copper and zinc
  2. B. two nonmetals, such as carbon and oxygen
  3. C. a metal and a nonmetal, such as sodium and chlorine
  4. D. a metal and a noble gas, such as zinc and argon
Show answer
Answer: B. two nonmetals, such as carbon and oxygen
Covalent bonds generally occur between two nonmetals, which share electrons rather than transferring them. Sodium with chlorine is a metal joined to a nonmetal, which gives an ionic bond; copper with zinc is two metals, which gives a metallic bond; and the noble gases already have a complete valence shell, so they generally form no compounds at all.
Question 2Beginner

An ionic bond forms when one atom steals electrons from another. Ionic bonds generally form between:

  1. A. two nonmetals
  2. B. a metal and a nonmetal
  3. C. a metal and a metalloid
  4. D. two metals
Show answer
Answer: B. a metal and a nonmetal
Ionic bonds generally form between metals and nonmetals: the metal loses valence electrons and the nonmetal gains them, so both reach a complete valence shell. Two nonmetals share electrons in a covalent bond and two metals form a metallic bond, while metalloids are set aside in a noted exception rather than used as a bonding rule.
Question 3Beginner

In a metallic bond, the valence electrons of the metal atoms are:

  1. A. transferred completely from one metal to another
  2. B. locked in place on one metal atom apiece
  3. C. delocalized among all of the metal atoms
  4. D. shared by exactly two metal atoms at a time
Show answer
Answer: C. delocalized among all of the metal atoms
A metallic bond forms from the attraction of positive metal ions and their negative sea of electrons, and the official name for those electrons is delocalized — they are free to move between any of the atoms in the whole piece of metal. Because they are not locked onto one atom, not handed over permanently, and not restricted to a pair of atoms, a metal can conduct electricity and be hammered into a new shape.
Question 4Beginner

Which of these substances is held together by ionic bonds?

  1. A. CO₂, carbon dioxide
  2. B. K₂S, potassium sulfide
  3. C. CH₄, methane
  4. D. N₂, molecular nitrogen
Show answer
Answer: B. K₂S, potassium sulfide
Potassium is a metal and sulfur is a nonmetal, so K₂S is the metal-plus-nonmetal pairing that gives an ionic bond. Carbon dioxide, molecular nitrogen, and methane are each built only from nonmetals, so every bond in them is covalent.
Question 5Beginner

An alloy such as brass is best described as:

  1. A. a mixture of metals joined by metallic bonds
  2. B. a metal and a nonmetal joined by ionic bonds
  3. C. two nonmetals that share electrons covalently
  4. D. a pure element that was melted and cooled again
Show answer
Answer: A. a mixture of metals joined by metallic bonds
An alloy is a mixture of metals, so brass — copper and zinc — is still held together by metallic bonding and still shows luster, malleability, conductivity, and ductility. A metal joined to a nonmetal would be an ionic compound, two nonmetals sharing electrons would be a covalent molecule, and a mixture of two elements is not a pure element no matter how it was heated.
Question 6Intermediate

Element X has a low electronegativity and a low first ionization energy. Element Y has a high electronegativity and a high first ionization energy. What type of bond forms between X and Y?

  1. A. Metallic, because the two elements pool electrons into a sea
  2. B. Covalent, because X gives up electrons that Y shares back evenly
  3. C. Covalent, because both elements hold their valence electrons tightly
  4. D. Ionic, because Y pulls electrons away from X to form ions
Show answer
Answer: D. Ionic, because Y pulls electrons away from X to form ions
A low electronegativity and a low first ionization energy mean X gives up electrons easily, while Y's high values mean Y pulls electrons in hard, so electrons transfer and oppositely charged ions attract. Saying both elements hold their valence electrons tightly describes two nonmetals sharing covalently, and a sea of pooled electrons requires two metals, which would mean both elements had low values.
Question 7Intermediate

In the Day 3 lab a copper penny is converted to brass, an alloy of copper and zinc. Which bond holds the brass together, and which property follows from it?

  1. A. Metallic bonds; delocalized electrons make the solid malleable
  2. B. Ionic bonds; the solid conducts electricity only when dissolved
  3. C. Covalent bonds; the atoms share pairs in fixed positions
  4. D. Metallic bonds; fixed electrons leave the solid brittle and dull
Show answer
Answer: A. Metallic bonds; delocalized electrons make the solid malleable
An alloy is a mixture of metals, and metal with metal gives a metallic bond in which the valence electrons become delocalized into a sea, which is what makes the sample malleable, ductile, conductive, and shiny. Electrons that were fixed in place would give a brittle, dull solid, and needing to be dissolved before conducting is the behavior of an ionic compound, not a metal.
Question 8Intermediate

A student tests three samples: potassium iodide (KI), carbon disulfide (CS₂), and a strip of magnesium metal. Which list gives the bond type in each, in that order?

  1. A. Covalent, ionic, metallic — nonmetal pair, metal with nonmetal, metal alone
  2. B. Ionic, metallic, covalent — metal with nonmetal, metal alone, nonmetal pair
  3. C. Metallic, ionic, covalent — metal alone, metal with nonmetal, nonmetal pair
  4. D. Ionic, covalent, metallic — metal with nonmetal, nonmetal pair, metal alone
Show answer
Answer: D. Ionic, covalent, metallic — metal with nonmetal, nonmetal pair, metal alone
Potassium is a metal bonded to the nonmetal iodine, so KI is ionic; carbon and sulfur are both nonmetals, so CS₂ is covalent; and a strip of magnesium is metal with metal, which is metallic. Calling CS₂ metallic or KI covalent ignores that bond type is read from where the elements sit on the table, not from how many atoms the formula contains.
Question 9Advanced

Day 3 predicts bond type from two trends: two elements that both have a high electronegativity and a high first ionization energy share electrons; when one element is low on both and the other is high on both, electrons are transferred; when both elements are low on both, the atoms pool their valence electrons. A table lists potassium as low/low, bromine as high/high, and copper and zinc each as low/low. What bond forms in potassium bromide, and what bond holds copper and zinc together in brass?

  1. A. Ionic in potassium bromide, because both elements are high on the two trends; covalent in brass, because both are low
  2. B. Covalent in potassium bromide, because both elements hold electrons tightly; metallic in brass, because both are low
  3. C. Ionic in potassium bromide, because one element is low and one is high; metallic in brass, because both are low
  4. D. Metallic in potassium bromide, because both elements are low on the trends; ionic in brass, because one is low and one is high
Show answer
Answer: C. Ionic in potassium bromide, because one element is low and one is high; metallic in brass, because both are low
Potassium is low on both trends and bromine is high on both, so bromine takes the electron and potassium bromide is ionic; copper and zinc are both low, so their valence electrons are delocalized into a shared sea and the bond is metallic. Calling potassium bromide covalent would require both atoms to hold their electrons tightly, which is false for potassium, and no pair of metals transfers electrons to make an ionic bond.
Question 10Advanced

In the alloy lab a copper penny is coated with zinc and then heated until the two metals mix into a brass surface layer. The layer conducts electricity, can be hammered flat without shattering, and does not melt on the hot plate. A student concludes that brass must be ionic, “because only ionic substances conduct and have high melting points.” What is the correct analysis?

  1. A. Brass is metallic; a sea of delocalized electrons carries current, lets layers slide, and holds a high melting point
  2. B. Brass is covalent molecular; electrons shared inside Cu–Zn molecules carry current and give it a high melting point
  3. C. Brass is ionic; copper ions and zinc ions attract in a lattice, which carries current and gives a high melting point
  4. D. Brass is a covalent network; every atom is locked into one giant molecule, which carries current and melts very high
Show answer
Answer: A. Brass is metallic; a sea of delocalized electrons carries current, lets layers slide, and holds a high melting point
An alloy is a mixture of two metals, so it is held together by metallic bonds: positive metal ions surrounded by delocalized electrons that carry current and let planes of ions slide past one another instead of shattering. High melting points are not exclusive to ionic solids — pure copper melts at 1085 °C and iron at 1538 °C. A dry ionic lattice does not conduct at all, and an alloy contains no discrete Cu–Zn molecules and no covalent network.

Ionic Bonding and Formulas

Question 11Beginner

The crisscross method starts by writing the oxidation numbers as superscripts. The next steps are to:

  1. A. multiply the two charges and use that product
  2. B. erase the charges, then swap them as subscripts
  3. C. keep the +/- charges and write them as subscripts
  4. D. add the two charges together and use that total
Show answer
Answer: B. erase the charges, then swap them as subscripts
The four crisscross steps are: write the oxidation numbers as superscripts, erase the +/- charges, swap the numbers to the other atom as subscripts, then reduce and drop any 1s. Adding or multiplying the two charges is not part of the method, and the charges are erased rather than carried down into the finished formula.
Question 12Beginner

Lithium forms Li¹⁺ and oxygen forms O²⁻. What is the formula for lithium oxide?

  1. A. LiO₂
  2. B. Li₂O₂
  3. C. LiO
  4. D. Li₂O
Show answer
Answer: D. Li₂O
Crisscrossing Li¹⁺ with O²⁻ gives Li₂O₁, and dropping the 1 leaves Li₂O, so two 1+ ions balance one 2- ion. LiO₂ puts the subscripts on the wrong atoms, LiO leaves a leftover negative charge, and Li₂O₂ adds an extra oxygen that nothing balances.
Question 13Beginner

Calcium forms Ca²⁺ and sulfur forms S²⁻. What is the formula for calcium sulfide?

  1. A. Ca₂S₂
  2. B. CaS
  3. C. Ca₂S
  4. D. CaS₂
Show answer
Answer: B. CaS
Crisscrossing Ca²⁺ with S²⁻ gives Ca₂S₂, and the last crisscross step says to reduce by dividing both subscripts by 2, which leaves CaS. Ca₂S₂ is the un-reduced answer, while CaS₂ and Ca₂S do not balance a 2+ charge against a 2- charge.
Question 14Beginner

Magnesium forms Mg²⁺ and nitrogen forms N³⁻. What is the formula for magnesium nitride?

  1. A. Mg₃N
  2. B. Mg₂N₃
  3. C. MgN
  4. D. Mg₃N₂
Show answer
Answer: D. Mg₃N₂
Crisscross moves the 3 from N³⁻ down onto magnesium and the 2 from Mg²⁺ down onto nitrogen, giving Mg₃N₂, in which three 2+ charges balance two 3- charges. Mg₂N₃ crisscrosses in the wrong direction, and MgN and Mg₃N both leave the total charge uneven.
Question 15Beginner

In a finished ionic compound, the total positive charge and the total negative charge must:

  1. A. be equal, so the compound is neutral overall
  2. B. be equal to the number of atoms in the formula
  3. C. add up to a small negative overall charge
  4. D. add up to a small positive overall charge
Show answer
Answer: A. be equal, so the compound is neutral overall
Ions combine in whatever ratio makes the positive and negative charges cancel, which is exactly what the crisscross method produces — in Al₂O₃ two 3+ ions balance three 2- ions. A completed ionic compound is neutral, so it cannot keep a leftover positive or negative charge, and the charges are not a count of atoms.
Question 16Intermediate

Aluminum forms Al³⁺ and sulfur forms S²⁻. Following the crisscross steps, what is the formula for aluminum sulfide?

  1. A. AlS, since the 3+ and the 2− simply cancel out
  2. B. Al₃S₂, since each charge stays on its own element
  3. C. Al₂S₃, since each charge becomes the other's subscript
  4. D. AlS₃, since only aluminum's charge is crisscrossed over
Show answer
Answer: C. Al₂S₃, since each charge becomes the other's subscript
Crisscrossing puts the 3 from Al³⁺ on sulfur and the 2 from S²⁻ on aluminum, giving Al₂S₃, which balances at 2(3+) = 6+ and 3(2−) = 6−. Writing AlS assumes unequal charges can cancel, Al₃S₂ leaves each number on the element it came from, and AlS₃ crisscrosses in only one direction.
Question 17Intermediate

Magnesium forms Mg²⁺ and oxygen forms O²⁻. A student crisscrosses the charges and writes Mg₂O₂. What should the final formula be?

  1. A. MgO₂, because only the oxygen subscript is reduced
  2. B. Mg₂O₂, because both ions carry a charge of two
  3. C. MgO, because the subscripts both reduce by 2
  4. D. Mg₂O, because only the magnesium subscript is kept
Show answer
Answer: C. MgO, because the subscripts both reduce by 2
Step 4 of the crisscross method says to reduce the subscripts by dividing by the same number and to drop any 1s, so Mg₂O₂ divided by 2 becomes MgO. Leaving Mg₂O₂ skips that step entirely, and reducing only one of the two subscripts, as in MgO₂ or Mg₂O, leaves the compound with a net charge instead of a neutral formula.
Question 18Intermediate

Calcium nitride has the formula Ca₃N₂. Which pair of ions produced that formula?

  1. A. Ca²⁺ and N³⁻, with the subscripts crisscrossed back
  2. B. Ca³⁺ and N²⁻, reading the subscripts straight across
  3. C. Ca²⁺ and N²⁻, since both ions come from the same period
  4. D. Ca¹⁺ and N¹⁻, since the compound is neutral overall
Show answer
Answer: A. Ca²⁺ and N³⁻, with the subscripts crisscrossed back
Reversing the crisscross, the 3 on calcium came from nitrogen's 3− and the 2 on nitrogen came from calcium's 2+, and the charges balance: 3(2+) = 6+ and 2(3−) = 6−. Reading Ca³⁺ and N²⁻ straight off the subscripts reverses the whole method, and neither 1+ and 1− nor 2+ and 2− would ever produce the subscripts 3 and 2.
Question 19Intermediate

In the Day 12 puzzle-piece activity, students combine lithium (Li) with phosphorus (P). How do the electrons move, and what formula results?

  1. A. Two lithium atoms each give up one electron, forming Li₂P
  2. B. Phosphorus gives up its three electrons to lithium, forming LiP
  3. C. Three lithium atoms each give up one electron, forming Li₃P
  4. D. One lithium atom gives up three electrons, forming LiP₃
Show answer
Answer: C. Three lithium atoms each give up one electron, forming Li₃P
Lithium has 1 valence electron and becomes Li¹⁺, while phosphorus needs 3 more electrons to complete its valence shell and becomes P³⁻, so it takes three lithium atoms to supply them, giving Li₃P. Lithium has only one valence electron to lose, so LiP₃ is impossible, and in an ionic bond the metal loses electrons to the nonmetal, not the other way around.
Question 20Intermediate

A student writes four ionic formulas from the oxidation numbers. Which formula is charge-balanced correctly?

  1. A. Ca₂O, built from calcium ions and oxide ions
  2. B. Mg₃N₂, built from magnesium ions and nitride ions
  3. C. K₂Cl, built from potassium ions and chloride ions
  4. D. AlBr₂, built from aluminum ions and bromide ions

Answer: B. Mg₃N₂, built from magnesium ions and nitride ions
Magnesium is Mg²⁺ and nitrogen is N³⁻, so Mg₃N₂ balances at 3(2+) = 6+ against 2(3−) = 6−. Potassium and chlorine are both 1, so the formula is KCl rather than K₂Cl; aluminum's 3+ needs three bromides, giving AlBr₃; and Ca²⁺ with O²⁻ reduces to CaO, not Ca₂O.
Question 21Advanced

A metal M forms an oxide with the formula M₂O₃. Oxygen's oxidation number in an oxide is 2−. Using the criss-cross method, what are the formulas of the compounds M forms with nitrogen (3−) and with sulfur (2−)?

  1. A. M is 2+, which gives M₃N₂ with nitrogen and MS with sulfur
  2. B. M is 1+, which gives M₃N with nitrogen and M₂S with sulfur
  3. C. M is 3+, which gives M₃N with nitrogen and MS₃ with sulfur
  4. D. M is 3+, which gives MN with nitrogen and M₂S₃ with sulfur
Show answer
Answer: D. M is 3+, which gives MN with nitrogen and M₂S₃ with sulfur
Three oxides at 2− total 6−, and two M atoms must balance that, so each M is 3+. Criss-crossing M³⁺ with N³⁻ gives M₃N₃, which reduces to MN, and M³⁺ with S²⁻ gives M₂S₃, already in lowest terms. A 2+ metal would have produced the oxide MO instead of M₂O₃, and M₃N or MS₃ leaves the charges unbalanced.
Question 22Advanced

In the ionic puzzle activity a group models beryllium with nitrogen. Beryllium has 2 valence electrons and gives up both; nitrogen has 5 valence electrons and gains 3 to complete its valence shell. What is the smallest whole-atom combination, and how many electrons move in total?

  1. A. Three beryllium atoms give up 6 electrons to two nitrogen atoms, giving Be₃N₂
  2. B. Two beryllium atoms give up 4 electrons to three nitrogen atoms, giving Be₂N₃
  3. C. Two beryllium atoms give up 6 electrons to three nitrogen atoms, giving Be₂N₃
  4. D. One beryllium atom gives up 2 electrons to one nitrogen atom, giving BeN
Show answer
Answer: A. Three beryllium atoms give up 6 electrons to two nitrogen atoms, giving Be₃N₂
Each beryllium releases 2 electrons and each nitrogen needs 3, so the count only balances at the least common multiple of 6: three Be²⁺ supplying 6 electrons to two N³⁻ accepting 6, which is Be₃N₂. A formula of Be₂N₃ would set a 4+ total against a 9− total, and BeN leaves nitrogen one electron short of a full valence shell.
Question 23Advanced

A student's worked solution shows two criss-crosses. Line 1: “Ca²⁺ and O²⁻ criss-cross to Ca₂O₂.” Line 2: “Al³⁺ and S²⁻ criss-cross to Al₂S₃, then reduce to AlS.” Which line, if either, needs fixing?

  1. A. Line 1 must reduce to CaO, and line 2 must be reduced one step further to AlS
  2. B. Both lines are correct, because criss-crossed subscripts are never reduced afterward
  3. C. Line 1 is correct as Ca₂O₂, and line 2 is correct once it is reduced to AlS
  4. D. Line 1 must reduce to CaO, while line 2 is already in lowest terms as Al₂S₃
Show answer
Answer: D. Line 1 must reduce to CaO, while line 2 is already in lowest terms as Al₂S₃
Step 4 of the criss-cross method divides the subscripts by a common factor, so Ca₂O₂ becomes CaO, but 2 and 3 share no common factor and Al₂S₃ stands as written. Reducing Al₂S₃ to AlS would pair a 3+ ion with a single 2− ion and leave the compound with a net charge, and leaving Ca₂O₂ keeps a ratio that is really 1:1.

Naming Ionic Compounds

Question 24Beginner

What is the name of MgO?

  1. A. magnesium (II) oxide
  2. B. magnesium oxygen
  3. C. magnesium oxide
  4. D. monomagnesium monoxide
Show answer
Answer: C. magnesium oxide
An ionic compound is named metal first, then the nonmetal with an -ide ending, so MgO is magnesium oxide. Prefixes such as mono- belong only to covalent molecules, magnesium is not a transition metal so it never takes a Roman numeral, and the nonmetal must be renamed oxide rather than left as oxygen.
Question 25Beginner

Which of these metals needs a Roman numeral in the names of its compounds?

  1. A. sodium
  2. B. iron
  3. C. aluminum
  4. D. zinc
Show answer
Answer: B. iron
Iron is a transition metal that can have more than one oxidation number, so its compounds are named iron (II) or iron (III) to show which charge the iron is using. Zinc, aluminum, and sodium each form only one common ion, so zinc chloride, aluminum oxide, and sodium chloride need no numeral.
Question 26Beginner

The copper ion in CuCl₂ has an oxidation number of 2+. What is the name of CuCl₂?

  1. A. monocopper dichloride
  2. B. copper chloride
  3. C. copper (I) chloride
  4. D. copper (II) chloride
Show answer
Answer: D. copper (II) chloride
Copper is a transition metal with more than one oxidation number, so the Roman numeral II states that the copper ion here is 2⁺. Copper (I) chloride is a different compound, CuCl; leaving the numeral off does not say which copper ion is present; and prefixes such as di- are never used in ionic names.
Question 27Beginner

Naming ionic compounds is easier than naming covalent molecules because ionic names:

  1. A. use a prefix only when there are three or more ions
  2. B. always end the metal's name with the -ide ending
  3. C. list the nonmetal first and then the metal second
  4. D. do not need prefixes to describe the number of ions
Show answer
Answer: D. do not need prefixes to describe the number of ions
The rule is that you do not need prefixes to describe the number of ions, because the balanced charges already fix the ratio — CaCl₂ is simply calcium chloride. The -ide ending goes on the nonmetal rather than the metal, the metal is always written first, and prefixes are not brought back for larger numbers of ions.

  2. B. FeO₃
  3. C. FeO
  4. D. Fe₃O₂

Answer: B. The metal ion is 1+, matching thallium (I), so thallium sulfate is Tl₂SO₄
No gel means the metal ion carries a 1+ charge, so the thallium present is thallium (I); balancing Tl¹⁺ against SO₄²⁻ takes two thallium ions, giving Tl₂SO₄. Question 139Advanced

A suspect stated in her interview that she wears white-gold earrings and is allergic to nickel. The earring found at the scene is an alloy of one gold-colored metal and one silver-colored metal. In testing, the gold-colored piece sits in silver nitrate solution with no change, while the silver-colored piece placed in copper (II) sulfate solution becomes coated with a reddish film. A metal replaces only another metal that is less reactive than itself, and copper is more reactive than silver. Which identification fits?

  1. A. Gold and silver; silver plates out of copper sulfate while gold reacts with nothing, matching an alloy
  2. B. Copper and silver; copper replaces silver while silver outranks copper, matching a nickel allergy
  3. C. Copper and nickel; copper fails to replace silver while nickel outranks copper, matching an allergy
  4. D. Gold and nickel; nickel outranks copper while gold cannot replace silver, matching a nickel allergy
Show answer
Answer: D. Gold and nickel; nickel outranks copper while gold cannot replace silver, matching a nickel allergy
Copper does replace silver, so a gold-colored metal that leaves silver nitrate unchanged cannot be copper — it is gold. The silver-colored piece pulled reddish copper out of copper (II) sulfate, so it is more reactive than copper, which fits nickel and rules out silver. White gold made of gold alloyed with nickel is exactly what the suspect described wearing.

Testing the victim's sugar bowl showed sucrose mixed with an ionic compound. Investigators want to know WHICH ionic compound. Which test would best narrow it down?

  1. A. Melt the sample again over a candle and time how long the crystals take to melt
  2. B. Repeat the conductivity test on the dissolved sample a second time for confirmation
  3. C. Weigh the dry sample on a balance and compare the mass with a pure sucrose sample
  4. D. Add a drop of Gaviscon to the dissolved sample and watch whether it gels
Show answer
Answer: D. Add a drop of Gaviscon to the dissolved sample and watch whether it gels
The Gaviscon test responds to the CHARGE of the metal ion — it gels with a 2+ ion and stays liquid with a 1+ ion — so it narrows the field in a way the earlier tests cannot.
