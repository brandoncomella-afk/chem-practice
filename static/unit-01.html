Intensive vs. Extensive Properties

Question 1Beginner

An intensive property is a property that:

  1. A. changes as the amount of substance changes
  2. B. always requires a chemical reaction to observe
  3. C. does not change as the amount of substance changes
  4. D. can only be measured in the lab
Show answer
Answer: C. does not change as the amount of substance changes
An intensive property stays the same no matter how much of the substance you have, which is exactly why it can be used to IDENTIFY a substance. Extensive properties change with the amount, so they cannot identify anything.
Question 2Beginner

Which of the following is an EXTENSIVE property?

  1. A. The sample is a blue crystal
  2. B. The sample has a mass of 7.1 grams
  3. C. The density of the sample is 2.7 g/mL
  4. D. The sample melts at 660.3 °C
Show answer
Answer: B. The sample has a mass of 7.1 grams
Take twice as much of the substance and the mass doubles, so mass depends on the amount present — that makes it extensive. Density, melting point, and color are all the same for any size sample.
Question 3Beginner

Which property could be used to IDENTIFY an unknown substance?

  1. A. Its temperature
  2. B. Its volume
  3. C. Its mass
  4. D. Its density
Show answer
Answer: D. Its density
Only intensive properties identify a substance, and density is intensive — every sample of the same substance has the same density. Mass and volume both change with sample size, and temperature simply describes how hot the sample is right now — none of the three says anything about identity.
Question 4Beginner

“The melting point of aluminum is 660.3 °C.” This statement describes:

  3. C. an extensive property
  4. D. an intensive property
Show answer
Answer: D. an intensive property
A gram of aluminum and a kilogram of aluminum both melt at 660.3 °C. Because the value does not depend on how much you have, melting point is intensive.

Accuracy vs. Precision

Question 15Beginner

Accuracy describes:

  1. A. how close repeated measurements of the same sample are to each other
  2. B. how many significant digits the measurement is reported with
  3. C. how close a measurement is to the accepted or true value
  4. D. how expensive the instrument is that produced the measurement
Show answer
Answer: C. how close a measurement is to the accepted or true value
Accuracy is a comparison to reality — the accepted value. Agreement among your own repeated trials is precision, and the two are completely independent of each other.
Question 16Beginner

Precision describes:

  1. A. how large the measured value is
  2. B. whether the substance is pure
  3. C. how close repeated measurements are to each other
  4. D. how close a measurement is to the accepted value
Show answer
Answer: C. how close repeated measurements are to each other
Precision is about reproducibility — do you get the same answer each time you repeat the measurement? A set of trials can be tightly clustered (precise) and still be far from the true value.

Answer: A. precise but not accurate
The three trials agree to within 0.03 mL of one another, so the data are precise. But all three sit about 1.57 mL below the actual value, so they are not accurate — a pattern that usually points to a problem with the instrument or the technique.

Measuring with Significant Digits

Question 27Beginner

When measuring a sample, the recorded measurement should include:

  1. A. the known digits and two estimated digits
  2. B. as many digits as the calculator displays
  3. C. the known digits and one estimated digit
  4. D. only the known digits
Show answer
Answer: C. the known digits and one estimated digit
You record every digit you are certain of from the instrument's markings, plus exactly ONE digit you estimate between the markings. Adding more estimated digits claims precision the instrument does not have.

Answer: B. the estimated digit
The final digit is the one you estimated between the markings, which is why every measurement carries some uncertainty. That estimated digit still counts as significant.

Calculating with Significant Digits

Question 45Beginner

When MULTIPLYING two measurements, the answer should have:

  1. A. as many digits as the calculator shows

Answer: D. the same number of significant digits as the number with the FEWEST significant digits
Your answer can never be more certain than your weakest measurement, so the value with the fewest significant digits sets the limit. Round up or down as needed to reach that count.
