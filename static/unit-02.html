History of Atomic Models

Question 1Beginner

John Dalton's atomic theory of 1803 described atoms as:

  1. A. solid particles that cannot be split apart
  2. B. positive spheres with electrons stuck inside
  3. C. mostly empty space around a dense nucleus
  4. D. clouds of electrons moving on fixed rings
Show answer
Answer: A. solid particles that cannot be split apart
Dalton pictured each atom as one solid particle with no smaller parts, which is why his postulates came long before the electron was even known. Mostly empty space around a dense nucleus is Rutherford's picture, electrons stuck in a positive sphere is Thomson's plum pudding, and electrons on fixed rings is Bohr's.
Question 2Beginner

In 1897 J. J. Thomson used a cathode ray tube and became the first scientist to:

  1. A. prove that atoms could not be divided
  2. B. discover the neutron inside the nucleus
  3. C. measure the energy of a bright line color
  4. D. discover the negatively charged electron
Show answer
Answer: D. discover the negatively charged electron
Thomson's cathode ray bent toward a positive plate, showing that the beam was made of negatively charged particles, which we now call electrons. The neutron waited for Chadwick in 1932, and it was Dalton who claimed atoms could not be divided.
Question 3Beginner

Rutherford's nuclear atom differs from Thomson's plum pudding model because Rutherford said the positive charge is:

  1. A. carried by the electrons themselves
  2. B. spread evenly throughout the atom
  3. C. packed into a tiny central nucleus
  4. D. found only on the outside surface
Show answer
Answer: C. packed into a tiny central nucleus
Rutherford concluded that all of the positive charge sits in a tiny, dense nucleus at the center of a mostly empty atom. Thomson had pictured that charge spread evenly through the whole atom, and electrons carry the negative charge, so they cannot be the source of the positive charge.
Question 4Beginner

Niels Bohr improved the model of the atom in 1913 by proposing that electrons:

  1. A. have no mass and no electric charge
  2. B. sit still on the surface of the nucleus
  3. C. are spread evenly through a positive ball
  4. D. travel in rings at set energy levels
Show answer
Answer: D. travel in rings at set energy levels
Bohr placed the electrons on rings, and each ring is a specific energy level, which explains why an element gives off only certain colors of light. Electrons spread evenly through a positive ball is Thomson's older model, and electrons do carry a negative charge and a very small mass.
Question 5Beginner

James Chadwick's 1932 discovery added which particle to the model of the atom?

  1. A. the nucleus
  2. B. the proton
  3. C. the neutron
  4. D. the electron
Show answer
Answer: C. the neutron
Chadwick identified the neutron, the neutral nuclear particle that explains why helium was known to have 2 protons but a mass of 4. Thomson had already found the electron in 1897, the proton was known before 1932, and the nucleus that Rutherford found in 1911 is a region, not a particle.
Question 6Beginner

Which list places these atomic models in the correct order, from earliest to most recent?

  1. A. Thomson, Dalton, Bohr, Rutherford
  2. B. Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr
  3. C. Bohr, Rutherford, Thomson, Dalton
  4. D. Rutherford, Bohr, Dalton, Thomson
Show answer
Answer: B. Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr
Dalton published in 1803, Thomson in 1897, Rutherford in 1911, and Bohr in 1913, so the solid-particle atom comes first and the ringed atom comes last. Any order that puts Bohr or Rutherford ahead of Dalton reverses more than a century of work.
Question 7Intermediate

One model of the atom shows positive material spread through the whole sphere with electrons stuck in it like raisins in dough. A newer model packs all of that positive charge into a tiny center with electrons in the space around it. Which piece of evidence forced the change from the older model to the newer one?

  1. A. Cathode rays inside a sealed glass tube bent toward the positively charged metal plate
  2. B. Each element gave off only a few specific colors of light in its own emission spectrum
  3. C. Two elements always combined with each other in the same whole-number mass ratios
  4. D. A few positively charged alpha particles bounced back off a thin sheet of gold foil
Show answer
Answer: D. A few positively charged alpha particles bounced back off a thin sheet of gold foil
Bouncing alpha particles back can only happen if the positive charge is concentrated in a tiny, dense center, which is what Rutherford concluded in 1911. The bending of cathode rays discovered the electron but said nothing about a nucleus, and fixed mass ratios only supported Dalton's much earlier indivisible-sphere picture.
Question 8Intermediate

Van Meegeren painted his suspected forgery in the 1930s. Which list places the models of the atom in the order they were accepted, starting with the earliest and ending with the model that was current while he painted?

  1. A. plum pudding, indivisible sphere, energy levels, nuclear atom, quantum mechanical
  2. B. indivisible sphere, plum pudding, nuclear atom, energy levels, quantum mechanical
  3. C. indivisible sphere, nuclear atom, plum pudding, quantum mechanical, energy levels
  4. D. nuclear atom, plum pudding, indivisible sphere, quantum mechanical, energy levels
Show answer
Answer: B. indivisible sphere, plum pudding, nuclear atom, energy levels, quantum mechanical
The dates run Dalton 1803 (indivisible sphere), Thomson 1897 (plum pudding), Rutherford 1911 (nuclear atom), Bohr 1913 (energy levels), then the quantum mechanical model of the 1920s. Any list that puts the nuclear atom before plum pudding reverses Thomson and Rutherford, and putting plum pudding first ignores Dalton entirely.
Question 9Intermediate

Dalton stated in 1803 that all atoms of a given element are identical, including their masses. Which later discovery showed that this postulate had to be revised, and why?

  1. A. Spectra, because atoms of one element release different colors of light at once
  2. B. Isotopes, because atoms of one element can hold different numbers of neutrons
  3. C. Ions, because atoms of one element can gain or lose protons and change mass
  4. D. Orbitals, because atoms of one element take up different volumes as they move
Show answer
Answer: B. Isotopes, because atoms of one element can hold different numbers of neutrons
Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 are both chlorine, both with 17 protons, yet their masses differ by 2 neutrons, so identical mass fails. Ions differ in electrons and never in protons, and neither the size of the electron cloud nor the set of emission lines has anything to do with the mass of the atom.
Question 10Intermediate

Your notes say that the quantum mechanical model is the current model of the atom but is incredibly difficult to draw, so the Bohr model is used to explain basic ideas. Which statement best explains why the Bohr model is still worth using?

  1. A. It counts the neutrons in the nucleus, which the quantum mechanical model hides away
  2. B. It replaces the nucleus with a spread-out positive pudding that is easy to sketch quickly
  3. C. Electrons sit in fixed energy levels, which explains why an element emits set colors
  4. D. It shows the exact position and the exact energy of every electron at the same moment
Show answer
Answer: C. Electrons sit in fixed energy levels, which explains why an element emits set colors
Bright lines exist because electrons can only sit at certain energy levels, and rings on a Bohr drawing show that idea clearly. Knowing both position and energy at once is exactly what Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle forbids, and no model of the atom hides the neutrons or brings back the plum pudding.
Question 11Intermediate

Helium was known to have 2 protons but a mass of 4 atomic mass units, a gap chemists could not explain until 1932. How did Chadwick's discovery close the gap?

  1. A. The 2 missing mass units come from the 2 energy levels that hold the electrons
  2. B. The 2 missing mass units come from 2 neutrons sitting in the nucleus
  3. C. The 2 missing mass units come from 2 extra electrons sitting in the nucleus
  4. D. The 2 missing mass units come from 2 extra protons hidden in the electron cloud
Show answer
Answer: B. The 2 missing mass units come from 2 neutrons sitting in the nucleus
A neutron carries about 1 atomic mass unit and no charge, so 2 protons plus 2 neutrons give a mass of 4 while the charge stays 2+. Extra protons would change helium into a different element, and electrons and energy levels contribute almost no mass at all.
Question 12Advanced

A student writes on the Atomic History Notes Summary: “Rutherford's gold foil experiment showed that electrons travel in fixed energy levels around the nucleus.” Which correction is needed?

  1. A. The foil experiment showed that atoms cannot split; fixed energy levels came from Chadwick and neutrons
  2. B. The foil experiment showed a small dense nucleus; fixed energy levels came from Dalton and his postulates
  3. C. The foil experiment showed the plum pudding atom; fixed energy levels came from Thomson and cathode rays
  4. D. The foil experiment showed a small dense nucleus; fixed energy levels came from Bohr and emission spectra
Show answer
Answer: D. The foil experiment showed a small dense nucleus; fixed energy levels came from Bohr and emission spectra
Most alpha particles passed straight through the foil and a few bounced back, which told Rutherford only that the positive charge and nearly all of the mass sit in a tiny nucleus. The idea that electrons occupy fixed energy levels came from Bohr in 1913, and his evidence was the bright-line emission spectrum of hydrogen — not scattered alpha particles.
Question 13Advanced

Lead white from a known Vermeer and lead white from the questioned painting both contain atoms with 82 protons, yet the two samples have different average atomic masses. Which of Dalton's postulates must be revised, and whose later discovery explained why?

  1. A. The postulate that atoms of an element cannot be divided; Rutherford's 1911 nucleus explained isotopes
  2. B. The postulate that all atoms of an element are identical; Thomson's 1897 electron explained isotopes
  3. C. The postulate that atoms combine in whole-number ratios; Bohr's 1913 energy levels explained isotopes
  4. D. The postulate that all atoms of an element are identical; Chadwick's 1932 neutron explained isotopes
Show answer
Answer: D. The postulate that all atoms of an element are identical; Chadwick's 1932 neutron explained isotopes
Both samples are lead because both are built from 82-proton atoms, so the only thing that can differ is the neutron count — these are isotopes, and Dalton's claim that every atom of an element is identical fails. Chadwick identified the neutron in 1932, which is what supplied the missing mass; Thomson's electron and Rutherford's nucleus say nothing about why two lead atoms would weigh differently.
Question 14Advanced

Which statement correctly names something a model could NOT explain?

  1. A. Thomson's model could not explain how a negatively charged particle could come out of any element
  2. B. Bohr's model could not explain why an electron's position and energy cannot both be known at once
  3. C. Dalton's model could not explain why atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios in a compound
  4. D. Rutherford's model could not explain why most alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil
Show answer
Answer: B. Bohr's model could not explain why an electron's position and energy cannot both be known at once
Bohr placed electrons in defined rings at defined energies, so his model has no room for Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle, which says the position and the energy of an electron cannot both be known. The other three describe exactly what each model was built to explain: whole-number ratios were Dalton's, straight-through passage was Rutherford's, and the electron was Thomson's own discovery.

Evidence and Experiments

Question 15Beginner

In the gold foil experiment, most alpha particles went straight through but a few bounced back at sharp angles. This is evidence that the atom contains:

  1. A. electrons packed tightly on the outside
  2. B. a tiny dense positive core in the center
  3. C. a large positive region filling the atom
  4. D. equal amounts of matter everywhere in it
Show answer
Answer: B. a tiny dense positive core in the center
Because almost every alpha particle passed through, the atom must be mostly empty space, and the rare sharp bounce means a few particles hit something small, heavy, and positive — the nucleus. A large positive region filling the atom would have deflected nearly every particle instead of just a few.
Question 16Beginner

In a cathode ray tube the beam bends away from a negative plate and toward a positive plate. This is evidence that the particles in the beam are:

  1. A. heavier than atoms
  2. B. positively charged
  3. C. negatively charged
  4. D. electrically neutral
Show answer
Answer: C. negatively charged
Opposite charges attract, so a beam pulled toward the positive plate must itself carry negative charge — these are electrons. Positively charged particles would have been pushed away from that plate, and a neutral beam would not have bent at all.
Question 17Beginner

In the hula-hoop model of the gold foil experiment, the Styrofoam ball at the center stands for:

  1. A. the electron cloud of the atom
  2. B. the nucleus of the atom
  3. C. one incoming alpha particle
  4. D. the whole sheet of gold foil
Show answer
Answer: B. the nucleus of the atom
The thrown ping pong balls act as the alpha particles, and the Styrofoam ball is the small, dense nucleus they can bounce off of. The hula hoop marks the outer edge of the atom where the electron cloud is, so balls that pass through the hoop show that the atom is mostly empty space.
Question 18Intermediate

In the hula-hoop model of Rutherford's experiment, a group throws 250 ping pong balls at the model. Three of them hit the Styrofoam ball and bounce back, and the other 247 pass through or beside the hoop. What percent bounced back, and what does that tell you about the atom?

  1. A. 12% bounced back, so the nucleus takes up most of the volume inside of the atom
  2. B. 1.2% bounced back, so the nucleus is spread evenly through the atom's whole volume
  3. C. 98.8% bounced back, so the nucleus and the electron cloud are about the same size
  4. D. 1.2% bounced back, so the nucleus takes up a very small part of the atom's volume
Show answer
Answer: D. 1.2% bounced back, so the nucleus takes up a very small part of the atom's volume
3 ÷ 250 = 0.012, and 0.012 × 100% = 1.2%, so almost everything sails through untouched. Reading 0.012 as 12% moves the decimal two places the wrong way, and 98.8% is the percent that missed, which would wrongly suggest a huge nucleus.
Question 19Intermediate

In the cathode ray tube simulation the beam always bends toward the positively charged plate, and it does this no matter which gas is sealed inside the tube. Which pair of conclusions does this evidence support?

  1. A. The rays carry negative charge, and the same particle is present in every element
  2. B. The rays carry no charge at all, and the particle is found only in metal elements
  3. C. The rays carry negative charge, and the particle has more mass than the whole atom
  4. D. The rays carry positive charge, and a different particle is present in every element
Show answer
Answer: A. The rays carry negative charge, and the same particle is present in every element
Bending toward the positive plate means the ray itself must be negative, since opposite charges attract. Getting the same particle from every gas shows electrons are in all elements, and Thomson also showed the particle has almost no mass compared with the atom.
Question 20Intermediate

The white paint from the known Vermeer gives bright lines for lead, silver, and antimony. The white paint from the possible forgery gives bright lines for lead only. Using the rule that all of the lines from the known must be present in the unknown for a match, what is the correct conclusion?

  1. A. The paints do match, because the lead lines show up in both of the samples
  2. B. No conclusion is possible, because spectra cannot identify metals in paint
  3. C. The paints do match, because extra lines are always allowed within a match
  4. D. The paints do not match, because the silver and antimony lines are missing
Show answer
Answer: D. The paints do not match, because the silver and antimony lines are missing
A match requires every line of the known sample to appear in the unknown, and two whole sets of lines are absent from the forgery's paint. Sharing the lead lines is not enough, and since every element has its own unique emission spectrum, spectra identify metals very well.
Question 21Advanced

In the hula-hoop model of the gold foil experiment, a group throws 400 ping-pong balls and 3 of them hit the Styrofoam ball at the center. The data sheet lists Rutherford's own hit rate as 0.0125%. What does the comparison say about the group's model?

  1. A. Their hit rate of 0.0075% is about 60 times below Rutherford's rate, so the classroom model is nearly exact
  2. B. Their hit rate of 7.50% is about 600 times Rutherford's rate, so the hula hoop should be made much smaller
  3. C. Their hit rate of 0.750% is about 60 times Rutherford's rate, so their Styrofoam nucleus is much too small
  4. D. Their hit rate of 0.750% is about 60 times Rutherford's rate, so their Styrofoam nucleus is much too large
Show answer
Answer: D. Their hit rate of 0.750% is about 60 times Rutherford's rate, so their Styrofoam nucleus is much too large
3 ÷ 400 = 0.0075, and × 100 gives a hit rate of 0.750%; dividing by Rutherford's 0.0125% gives 0.750 ÷ 0.0125 = 60.0. Hitting the center 60 times too often means the Styrofoam ball is far too big compared with the hoop — a real nucleus is a far smaller fraction of the atom's width than the model shows.
Question 22Advanced

Thomson ran his cathode ray tube with different gases inside and different metals as electrodes, and the beam always bent toward the positive plate by the same amount at a given voltage. Which conclusion is BEST supported by that repetition?

  1. A. Nearly all of the atom's mass sits in a tiny nucleus, so the atom is mostly empty space
  2. B. Every element contains the same negatively charged particle, so the atom is not indivisible
  3. C. Electrons are limited to fixed energy levels, so every element gives off its own colors
  4. D. Every element contains the same positively charged particle, so the atom is not indivisible
Show answer
Answer: B. Every element contains the same negatively charged particle, so the atom is not indivisible

Subatomic Particles

Question 23Beginner

Which list gives the correct charge of each subatomic particle?

  1. A. proton neutral, neutron positive, electron negative
  2. B. proton negative, neutron neutral, electron positive
  3. C. proton positive, neutron neutral, electron negative
  4. D. proton positive, neutron negative, electron neutral
Show answer
Answer: C. proton positive, neutron neutral, electron negative
Protons carry the positive charge, neutrons carry no charge at all, and electrons carry the negative charge. Swapping the proton and electron signs is the most common slip, but the nucleus is the positive region of the atom, so the proton must be the positive one.
Question 24Beginner

Where are the three subatomic particles located in an atom?

  1. A. protons and electrons in the nucleus, neutrons in the electron cloud
  2. B. protons and neutrons in the nucleus, electrons in the electron cloud
  3. C. protons in the nucleus, neutrons and electrons in the electron cloud
  4. D. protons and neutrons in the electron cloud, electrons in the nucleus
Show answer
Answer: B. protons and neutrons in the nucleus, electrons in the electron cloud
An atom has two regions: the nucleus holds the protons and neutrons, and the electron cloud around it holds the electrons. Placing the electrons in the nucleus would leave the center with no net positive charge, which contradicts the gold foil results.
Question 25Beginner

Compared with a proton, an electron has:

  1. A. almost no mass at all
  2. B. roughly twice the mass
  3. C. about the same mass
  4. D. exactly one half the mass
Show answer
Answer: A. almost no mass at all
A proton and a neutron each count as about one atomic mass unit, while an electron's mass is so small that it is ignored when finding the mass number. That is why we say the mass of the atom is in the nucleus.
Question 26Beginner

Two samples are made of atoms that have the same number of protons. This means the samples:

  1. A. must be the same element
  2. B. must have the same number of neutrons
  3. C. must have the same mass
  4. D. must be different elements
Show answer
Answer: A. must be the same element
Protons are the particles that define the type of element, so an identical proton count always means the same element. It does not force the same mass or the same neutron count, because isotopes of one element differ in exactly that way.
Question 27Beginner

In a neutral atom, the number of electrons is always equal to the number of:

  1. A. neutrons plus protons
  2. B. neutrons in the nucleus
  3. C. protons in the nucleus
  4. D. rings in the model
Show answer
Answer: C. protons in the nucleus
In a neutral atom the positive charges equal the negative charges, so electrons = protons. Neutrons carry no charge, so neither the neutron count nor the total of protons plus neutrons has any effect on the charge balance.
Question 28Intermediate

Question 29Intermediate

A student adds two electrons to an atom of copper and says, "Now I have made a brand new element." What is wrong with this claim?

Question 30Intermediate

A neutral atom of iron-56 has 26 protons. Counting protons, neutrons, and electrons together, how many subatomic particles does the atom contain, and how many of those sit in the nucleus?

  1. A. 112 particles in all, and 82 of them sit inside the nucleus
  2. B. 82 particles in all, and 56 of them sit inside the nucleus

  4. D. 56 particles in all, and 26 of them sit inside the nucleus

Answer: B. 82 particles in all, and 56 of them sit inside the nucleus
Neutrons = 56 − 26 = 30, and a neutral atom has 26 electrons, so 26 + 30 + 26 = 82 particles in all. The nucleus holds only the protons and neutrons, 26 + 30 = 56 of them, so answers naming 26 in the nucleus are counting protons alone.
Question 31Advanced

  1. A. Electrons have almost no mass, so nearly all of the mass sits in the tiny nucleus instead
  2. B. Electrons have almost no volume, so nearly all of the volume sits in the tiny nucleus instead

Volume and mass are different properties, and the student has traded one for the other. Question 32Advanced

Which comparison of the subatomic particles in a neutral ₈₂²⁰⁸Pb atom is correct?

  1. A. Its 82 protons and 126 neutrons each have about the same mass; only the protons are charged
  2. B. Its 126 neutrons and 82 electrons each have about the same mass; only the protons are charged

Reading the Periodic Table

Question 33Beginner

On the periodic table, the top number in each element box is the atomic number. It tells you the number of:

  1. A. isotopes of the element
  2. B. protons in the atom
  3. C. rings in the atom
  4. D. neutrons in the atom
Show answer
Answer: B. protons in the atom
The atomic number is defined as the number of protons, which is why it never changes for a given element. The neutron count is not printed on the table at all — you have to subtract the atomic number from a mass number to find it.
Question 34Beginner

The bottom number in a periodic table box is a decimal, such as 35.45 for chlorine, because it is:

  1. A. the mass number of the most common atom
  2. B. an average mass of all of the isotopes
  3. C. a count of the neutrons in a single atom
  4. D. the total of the protons and the electrons
Show answer
Answer: B. an average mass of all of the isotopes
Atomic mass is an average of the masses of all the isotopes of an element, weighted by percent abundance, and a weighted average rarely lands on a whole number. A mass number counts protons plus neutrons in one atom, so a mass number is always a whole number.
Question 35Beginner

You need the number of protons in an atom of cobalt. Which piece of information from the periodic table box do you use?

  2. B. the element symbol, Co
  3. C. the box position, period 4
  4. D. the atomic number, 27
Show answer
Answer: D. the atomic number, 27
The atomic number 27 is the proton count, so every cobalt atom has exactly 27 protons. The atomic mass 58.93 is an average that already includes neutrons, so it cannot give the proton count by itself.

Question 37Intermediate

Question 38Beginner

A carbon-14 atom has 6 protons. How many neutrons does it have?

  1. A. 8
  2. B. 6
  3. C. 14
  4. D. 20

Answer: A. 8
Mass number = protons + neutrons, so neutrons = 14 − 6 = 8. Answering 14 just repeats the mass number, and 20 comes from adding 14 + 6 instead of subtracting.
Question 39Beginner

How many protons, neutrons, and electrons are in a neutral neon-21 atom? Neon's atomic number is 10.

  1. A. 11 protons, 10 neutrons, 11 electrons
  2. B. 10 protons, 11 neutrons, 10 electrons
  3. C. 10 protons, 21 neutrons, 10 electrons
  4. D. 10 protons, 11 neutrons, 11 electrons

Answer: B. 10 protons, 11 neutrons, 10 electrons
The atomic number gives 10 protons, and neutrons = 21 − 10 = 11. The atom is neutral, so electrons equal protons at 10; a particle with 10 protons and 11 electrons would carry a 1− charge, and one with 11 protons is sodium, not neon.

Orbitals and the Quantum Model

Question 125Beginner

In the Quantum Mechanical Model, an electron orbital is best described as:

  1. A. a solid shell that traps the electrons inside
  2. B. the ring drawn in a Bohr model diagram
  3. C. the exact circular path of an electron
  4. D. the area electrons occupy 95% of the time
Show answer
Answer: D. the area electrons occupy 95% of the time
An electron orbital describes the area that a set of electrons can be found in 95% of the time, so it is a region of probability and not a path. Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle states that it is impossible to know both the energy and the position of an electron, which rules out any exact circular path.
Question 126Intermediate

An electron orbital is the region where a set of electrons is found 95% of the time; an s orbital holds 2 electrons, a p set holds 6, and a d set holds 10, and any electrons in the same orbital must have opposite spins. Which statement follows from these facts?

  1. A. A single orbital holds exactly 10 electrons, and all of them share the same spin
  2. B. A single orbital holds at most 2 electrons, and those 2 must have opposite spins
  3. C. A single orbital holds at most 6 electrons, as long as each pair has opposite spins
  4. D. A single orbital traces the exact circular path an electron follows around a nucleus
Show answer
Answer: B. A single orbital holds at most 2 electrons, and those 2 must have opposite spins
There are only two possible spins and electrons sharing an orbital must have opposite spins, so no orbital can hold more than 2 electrons; the 6 and the 10 are the totals for whole p and d sets. Describing an exact circular path is a Bohr ring, not an orbital, which is a region of probability.
Question 127Advanced

A set of d orbitals holds a maximum of 10 electrons, and any two electrons sharing one orbital must have opposite spins. How many separate d orbitals are in the set, and how do the spins divide?

  1. A. 10 orbitals, with 5 electrons of one spin and 5 of the other spin
  2. B. 5 orbitals, with 5 electrons of one spin and 5 of the other spin
  3. C. 5 orbitals, with all 10 electrons carrying one and the same spin
  4. D. 2 orbitals, with 5 electrons of one spin and 5 of the other spin
Show answer
Answer: B. 5 orbitals, with 5 electrons of one spin and 5 of the other spin
Each orbital holds at most 2 electrons, so a 10-electron set requires 10 ÷ 2 = 5 orbitals. Since the two electrons in each orbital must have opposite spins, a full set has 5 of one spin and 5 of the other; there are only two possible spins, so all 10 sharing one spin is impossible.
Question 128Advanced

A student says: “Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle means we cannot know how many electrons an atom has.” What does the principle actually say, and why does the student's version fail?

  1. A. An electron's position and energy cannot both be known; the electron count is therefore unknowable
  2. B. An electron's mass and charge cannot both be known; the electron count comes from the neutrons
  3. C. An electron's spin and charge cannot both be known; the electron count comes from the mass number
  4. D. An electron's position and energy cannot both be known; the electron count comes from the protons
Show answer
Answer: D. An electron's position and energy cannot both be known; the electron count comes from the protons
Heisenberg's principle limits knowing an electron's position and its energy at the same time — it says nothing about counting electrons. In a neutral atom the electron count equals the proton count, and in an ion it is the proton count adjusted by the net charge, so the number is always known exactly.
